009                                               A BSTRACT
010
011            Early anomaly warning requires both detectable evidence before event onset and
012            an alarm that exploits that evidence. Yet common evaluations define pre-onset
013            targets by temporal proximity and apply point adjustment, allowing reactive de-
014            tections to receive credit as apparent predictions. We term this failure mode the
015            precursor illusion. We introduce a model-agnostic, placebo-calibrated framework
016            that first tests whether pre-onset intervals are distinguishable from matched nor-
017
               mal operation, then tests whether frozen causal detector scores exploit the certi-
               fied evidence, and finally isolates evaluation effects while preserving scores and
018
               thresholds. The test detects evidence in 43 of 47 independently annotated transi-
019
               tions in the 3W dataset, yet across five benchmarks it identifies pre-onset devia-
020            tions in only 70 of 500 evaluable events, revealing sparse and dataset-dependent
021            evidence within the specified descriptor family. Mean detector scores achieve
022            above-chance discrimination between precursor and matched normal windows in
023            none of the 35 model–dataset combinations after multiple-testing correction, and
024            only the near-onset score peaks of LSTM-VAE separate SMD precursor windows
025            beyond a selection control, although all seven evaluated models distinguish the
026            3W transitions from normal operation. Point adjustment raises mean pre-onset
027            Shifted-warning F1 from 0.036 to 0.177 without changing any alarm time, and
028
               input-blind alarms at matched rates obtain higher adjusted F1 than every detector.
               These results show that benchmark warnability must be established independently
029
               before early-warning models are ranked.

032   1   I NTRODUCTION
033
034   Time-series anomaly detectors typically operate reactively and score deviations that are already
035   observable at or after event onset (Xu et al., 2022; Tuli et al., 2022; Song et al., 2023; Wang et al.,
036   2023; Feng et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2025; Zamanzadeh Darban et al., 2024).
037   Early-warning approaches instead aim to raise an alarm before the onset time te , using only the
038
      observations available at the alarm time, so that operators have time to intervene (You et al., 2024;
      Hu et al., 2024; Park et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2025). Such an alarm is possible only
039
      if the pre-onset interval contains observable evidence that distinguishes an approaching event from
040
      normal operation. Following work on early-warning signals for critical transitions, we refer to this
041   evidence as a precursor (Scheffer et al., 2009; Bury et al., 2021; 2023; Huang et al., 2024).
042
043   However, a common evaluation protocol labels the time steps just before each event onset as positive
044
      (Zhao et al., 2024; Kang et al., 2026). An alarm raised in this period then counts as an early warning,
      whether or not the data in the period differ from normal operation. The protocol thus assumes that
045
      every event is preceded by a precursor, without testing this assumption.
046
047   Another common evaluation practice further compounds this problem. Point adjustment counts an
048   entire positive segment as detected if an alarm occurs anywhere within it (Su et al., 2019; Zhao
049   et al., 2024; Lee et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2022; Wu & Keogh, 2023; Sarfraz et al., 2024; Wagner
050
      et al., 2023). When labels extend into the pre-onset period, a single post-onset hit can credit the
      entire extended segment. Figure 1 illustrates the resulting precursor illusion: a reactive detection
051
      can receive prediction credit without establishing both detectable precursor evidence and a pre-onset
052
      alarm.
053
      These observations motivate three research questions:

      The main contributions are summarized as follows:
081
082           • We formalize the distinction between precursor existence and alarm timing, and show why
083             extending anomaly labels into the pre-onset period and applying point adjustment are in-
084             sufficient to establish genuine early warning.
085           • We propose a model-agnostic, placebo-calibrated test for precursor detectability that uses a
086             prespecified descriptor family to evaluate whether pre-onset intervals at different lead times
087             contain evidence distinguishable from same-entity normal operation.
088           • We introduce matched-window precursor AUC (MPA) to assess whether frozen causal de-
089             tector scores exploit certified precursor evidence. We further develop a trace- and threshold-
090             preserving comparison that isolates the effects of evaluation targets and detection-credit
091             rules.
092           • We conduct the first systematic characterization of the warnability limits of existing early-
093             warning benchmarks, revealing sparse and dataset-dependent precursor evidence, score
094             separation confined to near-onset peaks and a separate per-machine setting, and system-
095             atic F1 inflation under point adjustment.

098
099   2.1     R EACTIVE T IME -S ERIES A NOMALY D ETECTION
100
101   Time-series anomaly detection identifies observations or intervals that deviate from normal behavior.
102
      Established families include isolation-based methods (Liu et al., 2008), reconstruction models (Park
      et al., 2018; Audibert et al., 2020), density-estimation approaches (Zong et al., 2018; Su et al.,
103
      2019), and temporal-association models (Xu et al., 2022). Recent work further develops recurrent
104
      and attention-based memory, temporal decomposition, cross-channel dependency modeling, and
105   frequency-domain representations (Tuli et al., 2022; Song et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023; Feng
106   et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2025; Zamanzadeh Darban et al., 2024).
107



                                                          2
      Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2027




108
      Despite architectural differences, most detectors remain reactive under standard online evaluation:
109   the score at time t reflects observations available only up to that time. They can establish detectabil-
110   ity once anomalous manifestations emerge, but performance near or after onset does not show that a
111   usable warning signal existed beforehand. Forecast-error detectors face the same limitation because
112   prediction errors can be computed only after the target observation arrives. Conventional detection
113   accuracy therefore does not establish pre-onset warnability.
114
115   2.2   E ARLY A NOMALY WARNING AND P RECURSORS
116
      Early anomaly-warning methods instead infer future abnormality from pre-onset observations. Ex-
117
      isting approaches include precursor detection, modeling of warning delays and horizons, forecasting
118   future observations or anomaly states, and learning pre-onset representations (You et al., 2024; Zhao
119   et al., 2024; Hu et al., 2024; Kang et al., 2026; Park et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2025).
120   Some methods derive warnings from forecasts, whereas others construct event-relative targets by
121   extending anomaly labels before onset or labeling sampled pre-onset windows as positive.
122
      Research on critical transitions shows that rising autocorrelation, increasing variance, and learned
123
      dynamical signatures may precede certain state changes (Scheffer et al., 2009; Bury et al., 2021;
124   2023; Huang et al., 2024). However, their presence, form, and detectable horizon are system-
125   dependent. Early-warning targets are often defined by temporal position, while their distinguisha-
126   bility from normal operation is not independently verified. Performance on such targets therefore
127   does not necessarily demonstrate detectable precursor evidence.
128
129   2.3   E VALUATION M ETRICS AND B ENCHMARK VALIDITY
130
      Time-series anomaly-detection evaluation has received increasing scrutiny. Pointwise F1 poorly rep-
131
      resents interval-valued anomalies, whereas point adjustment may substantially inflate performance
132
      by counting an entire segment as detected after a single hit (Kim et al., 2022; Sarfraz et al., 2024;
133   Wagner et al., 2023). Range-, affiliation-, volume-, soft-matching-, and proximity-based metrics
134   have been proposed to capture overlap, fragmentation, tolerance, and detection delay (Huet et al.,
135   2022; Paparrizos et al., 2022a; Ghorbani et al., 2024; Salles et al., 2024; Sørbø & Ruocco, 2024).
136   These metrics refine how predictions are counted once a target interval is accepted, but do not estab-
137   lish whether that interval contains detectable information.
138
      Benchmark studies have also identified duplicated or trivial segments, contaminated training data,
139   inconsistent annotations, and unstable model rankings (Wu & Keogh, 2023; Liu & Paparrizos, 2024;
140   Paparrizos et al., 2022b; Wagner et al., 2023). Their focus, however, remains benchmark reliability
141   after abnormal behavior becomes observable. Whether designated pre-onset intervals contain de-
142   tectable evidence before the annotated event begins remains insufficiently examined, limiting the
143   interpretation of both model performance and evaluation metrics in early anomaly warning.
144
145   3     M ETHODOLOGY
146
147   Our evaluation follows three stages aligned with RQ1–RQ3. Benchmark evidence is tested first,
148   frozen scores are then evaluated on the resulting event strata, and evaluation targets and scoring
149   rules are finally varied while scores, normalization, and thresholds remain fixed. This ordering
150   keeps the evidence for benchmark warnability independent of model performance.

245   3.4   RQ3: T RACE -P RESERVING P ROTOCOL C OMPARISON
246
247   In post hoc detection, point adjustment tolerates small misalignments between an annotated anomaly
248   segment and the alarms raised inside it (Su et al., 2019; Kim et al., 2022). Within a post-onset seg-
249
      ment, a single alarm shows that the detector responded to the anomaly, which makes this tolerance
      appropriate for detection. Our concern is the transfer of credit across the onset. A test-supported
250
      warning requires both De = 1 and an alarm in Pe ; extending a target to Pe ∪ Ae alone establishes
251
      neither condition. Under PA on the merged target Pe ∪ Ae , an alarm raised after the anomaly has
252   begun marks the preceding interval as warned. RQ3 evaluates PA only as a credit rule for early
253   warning.
254
255
      To isolate these effects, RQ3 freezes scores, normalization, and normal-only thresholds. The De-
      tection target is Ae , the Shifted-warning target is the disjoint Pe , and the Merged target is Pe ∪ Ae .
256
      Each PA variant expands a hit only within its own target, and every protocol is compared with input-
257
      blind alarms drawn at each detector’s own alarm rate. Detection and Shifted-warning PA measure
258   within-segment inflation; Merged PA directly tests whether post-onset hits receive pre-onset credit.
259   Thus target choice and credit assignment change, but detector outputs do not. Appendix A gives the
260   full definitions and failure-mode analysis.

461   5   L IMITATIONS AND F UTURE W ORK
462
463   A negative result does not rule out predictive information outside the specified descriptor family; a
464   positive result indicates a deviation from the normal reference, not necessarily a fault-specific pre-
465   cursor. The test applies to entities whose normal operation is stationary. Entities that switch between
466   operating modes require placebo windows matched to the current mode, as the drift of normal op-
467   eration before the annotated 3W transitions in Appendix B.1 illustrates. The seven detectors were
468   trained on pooled entities, and the per-machine experiment for iForest on SMD changes training,
469
      the normal reference, and the selected events together. Future work will separate these three factors,
      extend the descriptor set, develop mode-aware placebo matching, evaluate additional benchmarks
470
      with explicit transition annotations, combine MPA with event-level timeliness measures, and extend
471
      the test from onset-aligned windows to continuous scanning of all endpoints.

      6   C ONCLUSION
475
      Early warning requires detectable pre-onset evidence and a pre-onset alarm. The placebo-calibrated
476
      test identifies deviations in 70 of 500 eligible events across five benchmarks and 43 of 47 indepen-
477   dently annotated 3W transitions; 223 of 293 benchmark events with detected changes are positive
478   only on post-onset support. Near-onset peaks and a separate per-machine SMD setting reveal sep-
479   aration missed by the primary mean-score comparison. With scores and thresholds fixed, PA raises
480   warning F1 without improving alarm timing or precursor discrimination; it adds little on 3W, where
481   detectors already alarm before onset. Evaluation should characterize benchmark warnability under
482   an explicit evidence definition before model comparison and jointly assess precursor discrimination
483   and alarm timeliness. The framework separates precursor existence, detector response, and credit
484   for pre-onset alarms.
