# General Response

We thank the three reviewers for their careful reading. Reviewer $\color{red}{\text{Tery}}$ found the paper readable and well structured, Reviewer $\color{blue}{\text{wxGK}}$ valued the separation of three questions often conflated in early-warning evaluation and the controlled isolation of credit transfer across onset, and Reviewer $\color{green}{\text{pSK8}}$ found the problem interesting and understudied, with research questions that are nicely posed. Below we summarize the points raised by more than one reviewer and the new analyses behind our answers. Each reviewer's thread gives the full tables, and the revised PDF reports all of them.

*Line, table and section numbers refer to the submitted PDF, and appendix sections to the revised PDF.*

# 1. Validity of the precursor detectability test
Reviewers wxGK and pSK8 asked whether the test detects precursors rather than normal variation or annotation offsets, and how its choices are validated. Four new analyses address this.

**1. Local normal reference on 3W.** With the placebo null built only from the last 300, 900 or 1800 seconds of normal operation before each transition, the test detects 45, 45 and 43 of all 47 3W transitions, against 43 in the submission (App. B.1.2).

**2. Counterfactual ablation.** Replacing the level and linear trend before each 3W fault by those of normal operation of the same well lowers the detections from 43 to 0 and moves nine detection, prediction and classification models toward chance, for example the AUROC of USAD from 0.972 to 0.626 (App. B.1.4).

**3. Alternative detection mechanisms.** Under the same windows and calibration, Online Kernel CUSUM, MMDEW and TS2Vec detect 40, 33 and 42 of the 47 3W transitions and 42, 32 and 63 of the 500 benchmark events, against 43 and 70 for our test at similar false positive rates (App. B.1.3).

**4. Annotation offsets on PSM and SWaT.** The onset-continuity check classifies 5 of the 7 PSM and 7 of the 9 SWaT precursor-positive events as boundary shifts, and its SMD counts reproduce Tab. 8 (App. B.2.3).

# 2. Interpretation of the model results and of MPA
Reviewers wxGK and pSK8 asked what non-significance excludes, how aggregation and training affect the results, and whether MPA is the right measure.

**1. Aggregation and training.** With scores, events and controls fixed, the maximum over the final 50 steps raises the MPA of LSTM-VAE from 0.561 to 0.750, a paired change of +0.189 [0.119, 0.258], while per-machine training under the same budget changes neither iForest nor LSTM-VAE (App. B.4.3 and App. B.4.5).

**2. Excluded effect sizes.** On SMD the largest upper 95% bound of mean-score MPA is 0.656 with event-level and 0.669 with machine-level resampling. These bounds constrain effects such as an MPA of 0.70 but leave values around 0.60 open, and no detector falls inside the near-chance range [0.45, 0.55] (App. B.4.1).

**3. MPA and existing metrics.** In a controlled comparison only MPA has a fixed chance level of 0.5, whereas Affiliation-F1 reaches 0.653 with random scores, and Window-F1 drops from 0.800 to 0.167 when the ratio of normal to precursor windows grows from 1:1 to 20:1 while MPA stays at 0.750. On 3W, Range-F1, Affiliation-F1 and VUS restricted to the time before the fault complement MPA (App. B.5.5 and App. B.5.6).

# 3. Presentation
Reviewers Tery and pSK8 pointed out terms used before their definition and decisions stated without their reason. The revision defines onset, placebo calibration, the rejection rate with its formula and both exchangeability assumptions with references at their first use, gives the regularized covariance below Eq. 1, removes the wording causal detector, answers each research question at the end of its results, explains the five morphologies shown in Fig. 3(a), and states the eligibility criterion behind the 500 of 537 events. It also adds DeviceWarning-162, a new dataset of 162 events on 48 devices, as supplementary material described in App. C.
